*After reading "Good Words Better Than Gifts That Bring You 50 Centimeters Closer" (『50센티 더 가까워지는 선물보다 좋은 말』)*

**What kind of person do we want to contact again, and meet and talk with?**

For a long time, talking with people was a difficult subject for me.

It was especially hard to say kind words, to comfort the other person, and to say things that lift their mood.

If the words didn't come sincerely from my heart, they felt like empty flattery, and that made me uncomfortable. Even with people I sincerely wanted to comfort and encourage, I didn't know how to speak to them. I would listen to the other person's worries and offer a solution, or say what I thought were words of empathy, but it didn't seem to bring them much comfort.

To someone like me, this book gave three important realizations.

1. **Speak in a way that makes the other person the main character**
2. **Notice small things, and express specific interest and praise**
3. **Talk with 'people,' not things and places, at the center of the conversation**

## A Conversation Needs a Person at Its Center

Looking back, the reason I couldn't offer enough comfort until now, and the reason it was hard for relationships to grow deeper, may be that I wasn't having conversations focused on the other person.

When someone has a worry or is going through a hard time, and what they want is comfort rather than a solution, of course I should listen to their story. But I think I focused on solving the problem rather than on the other person's feelings.

It was the same in everyday conversation.

When I met people, I mostly talked about things, places, work, and careers. Of course, you can keep a conversation going with those topics alone. But after the conversation ended, it felt like we had been skirting around something, and it was hard to become closer to the other person as human beings.

We can talk about the same place without asking what the other person felt there. We can be interested in someone's work and career and still miss what state of mind they are living in.

**I realized that being interested in the subject of a conversation is different from being interested in the person themselves.**

Of course, a good conversation isn't made by my effort alone. Even if I try to center the conversation on the other person, we can only grow closer if they also take an interest in me and join in the conversation.

Whenever a conversation failed to deepen, I used to wonder whether something was wrong with the way I talk, but since a relationship is ultimately something two people build together, my thinking has changed a little on that point too.

## Stingy with Praise

Another thing I realized is that I am more stingy with praise than I thought.

I often feel uncomfortable when I praise someone. If I say kind words without having enough interest or admiration for the other person, it feels like flattery rather than sincerity.

In particular, I thought that when the other person is in the wrong or mistaken about something, or when they are seeking comfort, saying only nice things unconditionally could actually be poison.

Sometimes it even seemed that I was praising not for the other person's sake but for my own benefit, because I wanted to look like a good person. So there were times I felt it was better not to praise at all.

But reading the book changed my thinking about praise a little.

**Praise may not be about creating strengths that don't exist, but about noticing and expressing good qualities in the other person that I hadn't yet discovered.**

If I pay more attention to the other person, remember small changes, and find their good points in specific ways, I should be able to give sincere praise without needing to make anything up.

In that sense, what I may need to change is not simply how often I praise, but the attitude with which I look at the other person.

## But Can I Be Interested in Everyone?

Even so, there is still a worry that remains unresolved.

To have these kinds of conversations, I basically have to be interested in the other person. But interest doesn't arise naturally toward everyone. Sometimes I'm not curious about the other person, and sometimes I don't want to deepen the relationship.

Even in those situations, should I consciously take an interest in order to say kind words? Wouldn't asking questions I'm not truly curious about be just another form of formal conversation?

I haven't found the answer to this question yet.

But one thing has become clear.

Until now, in order to say good words, I worried about what words to say. I thought about how to comfort, how to praise, and how to keep a conversation going.

But perhaps what matters isn't the skill of speaking well, but how much interest I have in the other person.

This book let me look at a worry I've carried about conversation for a long time from a new perspective.

From now on, rather than forcing myself to come up with kind words, I want to observe the other person a little more and try to listen closely to their story.

**If you want to say many kind words, first take an interest in the other person.**
